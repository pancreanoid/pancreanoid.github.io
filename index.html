Md Abu Talha Siddique · Doctoral candidate, Department of Pharmaceutics, University of Florida

Tracing the first steps of pancreatic cancer in human acinar cells.

I study acinar-to-ductal metaplasia, the identity shift in which digestive-enzyme-producing acinar cells take on duct-like features. It is one of the earliest events on the road to pancreatic cancer. My work combines human pancreatic acinar organoids, transcriptomics and epigenetics with machine learning to understand how and why this transition begins.

Research areas

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is usually found late, which is why the earliest, pre-cancerous events matter so much. My doctoral research focuses on one of them, acinar-to-ductal metaplasia, and on building human cell models and computational tools to study it.

Pancreatic cancer initiation

Ongoing research

Acinar-to-ductal metaplasia

Acinar cells can be pushed to lose their secretory identity and adopt a ductal-like programme. I study what drives that plasticity and how it can be measured in human cells rather than only in mouse models.

Ongoing research

Human pancreatic acinar organoids

Three-dimensional cultures grown from human pancreatic tissue give a human-specific model of the acinar compartment. I use them to follow the acinar-to-ductal transition under controlled conditions.

Ongoing research

Transcriptomics and epigenetics

Gene expression and chromatin state record how a cell's identity is maintained or lost. I use transcriptomic and epigenetic profiling to characterise acinar states and the changes that accompany metaplasia.

Training and prior work

What metaplasia looks like

In a healthy acinus, pyramidal cells packed with enzyme-filled granules surround a tiny lumen. During acinar-to-ductal metaplasia the granules are lost, the cells flatten, the lumen widens and duct-associated genes switch on. The schematic is a simplification; real tissue is messier.

Acinar programmePTF1A, CPA1, amylase: high, then falling
Ductal programmeSOX9, KRT19: low, then rising

Marker behaviour shown here reflects the general literature on acinar-to-ductal metaplasia, not a result from my own experiments.

Machine learning for acinar subtype classification

Acinar cells are not a single uniform population. I am building classifiers that read transcriptomic profiles and assign cells or samples to acinar subtypes and states, so that the earliest steps toward a ductal-like identity can be described quantitatively.

Ongoing researchMethods and results are in development and have not yet been peer-reviewed or independently validated.

  1. Human acinar organoids

    Culture human pancreatic acinar organoids and expose them to defined conditions that promote or restrain metaplasia.

  2. Transcriptomic profiling

    Measure gene expression to capture acinar, intermediate and ductal-like states across conditions and time.

  3. Feature engineering

    Normalise, filter and select informative genes and gene programmes; integrate epigenetic features where available.

  4. Classification

    Train and cross-validate supervised models that assign acinar subtype or state labels, with attention to class balance and leakage.

  5. Interpretation

    Inspect which features drive the predictions and test whether they correspond to known biology, then design the next experiment.

The goal is a reproducible way to describe where a cell sits between a fully acinar and a ductal-like identity. That matters for comparing experimental conditions, for interpreting human samples and for asking which molecular changes come first.

Model choice is driven by data size and interpretability. Regularised linear models and tree-based ensembles are the first pass; deeper architectures are considered only where the data support them. Every model is evaluated on held-out data and reported with its limitations.

What this work is and is not

  • A research tool for classifying cell states in experimental and transcriptomic data.
  • Not a diagnostic test, and not validated for any clinical use.

About

From pharmacy and manufacturing to cancer biology and computation.

I am Md Abu Talha Siddique, a doctoral candidate in the Department of Pharmaceutics at the University of Florida College of Pharmacy. My research asks how pancreatic cancer gets started, with a focus on acinar-to-ductal metaplasia in human pancreatic acinar organoids and on the transcriptomic and epigenetic changes that accompany it.

I trained first as a pharmacist, earning a Bachelor of Pharmacy from Khulna University in Bangladesh, and then worked in pharmaceutical manufacturing before returning to research. A Master of Science in Biology from Tuskegee University moved me toward cell and molecular biology, and my doctoral work at Florida brought those threads together with quantitative pharmacology and machine learning.

The name of this site, PANCREANOID, joins pancreas and organoid: the human model system at the centre of my work.
